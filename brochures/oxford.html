University of Oxford — Computer Science and Philosophy (BA / MCompPhil)

Program Dossier · The Admission Book · Fall 2027 Entry · Research date: August 16, 2026

Oxford’s Computer Science and Philosophy degree is the closest thing either ancient university offers to this book’s central thesis — study minds, machines, and the reasoning that connects them inside one coherent course. Year 1 pairs algorithms and probability with a philosophy paper literally titled “Alan Turing on computability and intelligence”; by Year 3 the split between machine learning and philosophy of mind can run 25–75 in either direction. Three facts govern the decision: an applicant may apply to Oxford or Cambridge, not both, in the same year; the required TMUA admissions test must be booked by September 28, 2026 — before the October 15 UCAS deadline; and Oxford offers no need-based aid to US undergraduates — the course is financed by family funds and US federal loans. US applicants succeeded at about 6.5% over 2022–24.

2. The big picture — what kind of place this is

Oxford’s undergraduate education runs through the tutorial: one to three students, one expert, every week, defending prepared work out loud. Admission is built to find people who will thrive in that room, and the university says so with unusual bluntness — “Tutors at Oxford are only interested in your academic ability and potential,” and extracurricular activities count “only in so far as they demonstrate the selection criteria for your course.” This is the inverse of US holistic admissions: no activities résumé, no leadership narrative, no community-impact essay. One subject, argued deeply, is the whole game.

Two structural rules follow. First, applicants choose one course before applying and are examined against it — there is no undeclared year. Second, the application runs on the UK calendar: UCAS closes October 15, 2026 at 6pm UK time, the admissions test is sat in mid-October, shortlisted candidates interview online in December, and every decision lands on January 12, 2027 — before most US regular decisions are even read.

3. The program — Computer Science and Philosophy

The degree is jointly owned by the Department of Computer Science and the Faculty of Philosophy, and it is designed around the question this book’s applicant profile keeps asking: what is intelligence, in machines and in minds, and how should reasoning about it work?

  • Year 1. Computer science: functional programming, imperative programming, algorithm design, discrete mathematics, probability. Philosophy: introduction to logic, general philosophy, and “Alan Turing on computability and intelligence” — the foundations of machine intelligence as a first-year paper.
  • Year 2. Core CS (~25%): models of computation, algorithms, a group design practical. CS options (~25%): artificial intelligence, compilers, databases, logic and proof. Philosophy (~50%): ethics, knowledge and reality, philosophy of mind, philosophy of science.
  • Year 3. The split is elective, 25–75 either way. CS side: machine learning, computer-aided formal verification, quantum information. Philosophy side: philosophy of cognitive science, philosophical logic, and an optional philosophy thesis.
  • Year 4 (optional MCompPhil). Advanced options (computer vision, geometric deep learning) plus advanced philosophy and a thesis. All students apply for the four-year course and decide in Year 3 whether to take the fourth year.

The Institute for Ethics in AI lives inside the same Philosophy Faculty that teaches half this degree — its lunchtime seminars, the Philosophy, Law & Politics Colloquium, and annual lectures are open events an undergraduate can simply walk into.

4. How the pieces fit together

The profile this book is built around wants computation and the study of minds and the institutions that govern intelligent systems — usually assembled from three departments. Here it is one course: the technical spine is real (TMUA-gated, algorithms-first), the philosophy half carries ethics, mind, and cognitive science, and the eventual application domain — law — is served the standard UK way: any strong degree feeds a US JD or a UK law conversion afterward. The honest gap: there is no named law/policy paper inside the course; the governance thread is built from ethics papers, the thesis option, and the Institute’s orbit.

6. Beyond the classroom — the violin at Oxford

Admission ignores performance; life does not. The Oxford University Orchestra and Philharmonia audition at the start of each term (a 3–4 minute unaccompanied solo plus sight-reading, open to any subject). More than a dozen colleges offer instrumental awards and exhibitions to students of any degree — Merton (£300, Grade 8+ standard), New College (£300, recital-based), St John’s (£120/term of lessons + £400), Trinity, LMH, Worcester among them — typically auditioned in the first weeks of Michaelmas term. Choosing a college with a strong music society and instrumental award is the single best lever for a serious violinist here.

7. Access, feasibility, and honest risks

  • One Oxbridge slot. Oxford’s own 2027-entry guide: “you can’t apply to Oxford and Cambridge in the same year.” This dossier and the Cambridge dossier describe mutually exclusive applications.
  • The test comes first. TMUA booking closes September 28, 2026 (6pm UK); the test is sat October 12–16. Registration is the real application deadline.
  • Money. Overseas course fee £62,820 (2026/27) plus £12,600–18,900 living: roughly $96–104k per year, with no Oxford need-based aid for US undergraduates (Crankstart and Oxford Bursaries are home-status only; Reach Oxford excludes US nationals). US federal Direct/PLUS loans are usable. The 3-year BA partially offsets the sticker.
  • Mathematics culture. 96% of recent CS-family offer-holders took Further Maths; the department’s US benchmark names Calculus BC — an applicant holding Calculus AB should query the department before applying.
  • The odds. 10% course-wide, ~6.5% for US applicants university-wide, intake of 14. This is a reach on any list.
