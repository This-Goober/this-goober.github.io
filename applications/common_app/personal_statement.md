MOST RECENT ONE

If my notes app was in the physical world, it would be like walking through a contemporary art
exhibit: MBTI catalog, recordings of philosophical conversations with Dad, how I would teach my
future child emotional regulation in a hypothetical scenario, jotted-down video reel like “play and
edit ‘Hey Jude’ in all 12 keys on top of each other” or having a series explaining how practicing
with drone waves works.

I chose the notes app as an artifact to store all of that because I didn’t feel I had anywhere to
put it. Instead, I had another identity to maintain.

At school, I ran on my own principle of letting my utility speak for itself. Watching interests so
quickly become positions, organizations, and accomplishments made me wary of turning my
own interests into projects before I understood what purpose they were meant to serve. So in
orchestra, I proactively made it regular to write section bowings, whisper rehearsal notes to
classmates, and, when our teacher was unexpectedly absent, gave the class direction. And with
violin, I had started later than many musicians, but progressed quickly enough to be selected
into an advanced piano trio.

But after a while, what just came easily began taking more work to maintain. I had to practice a
disproportionate amount to meet the increasingly demanding expectations of each performance
opportunity and not let my trio mates down. At school, the qualities I trusted in myself—being
engaged, assertive, eager to contribute—were not always received the way I intended. I heard
myself being named a teacher’s pet through word of mouth, affecting my friendships and social
circle, and as I grew more insecure, I kept trying to appease the possibly valid criticism. The
harder I tried to hold together the person I thought I was supposed to be, the less certain I
became that I was actually succeeding at it.

Then a “Thank you for your commitment, but… ” letter arrived in my inbox, reassigning me to a
different group while my two other Trio mates were kept together and even assigned another
violinist. My closest friendships came to the last straw and I was then isolated and alone. Friday
evening rolls around, and my Dad had asked how I had been doing. Catching everyone off
guard, even myself, tears rolled down my face. I was deeply lost. I lost people and opportunities
I cared about, along with the evidence I had used to reassure myself that I was talented,
well-intentioned, and on track.

Rebuilding meant putting myself out there, making new connections, and learning to encourage
myself without relying on accomplishments to give me confidence.
But through rebuilding myself, I saw that in striving to maintain this “authentic” ideal persona, I
had hyper focused it to only being what achievements I earned and the behavior I displayed, not
making room for what I believed and what I wanted. In reality, authenticity does not require a
perfect narrative. As I no longer felt I had to preserve the old version of myself, I felt free to act
on what I actually cared about.

The questions that had stayed in my head became conversations. I collaborated with new
peers, spending hours mapping out our old vision of orchestra instruction we wished we had
received in middle school: how to structure a curriculum, which schools we could reach, and
how peer mentors could teach. I brainstormed ways to systematically produce all the video
ideas I had.

The questions that once stayed in my head became conversations, then collaborations, then
things I could finally put into the world.
